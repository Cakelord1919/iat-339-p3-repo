Designing urgency in three days

The Last of the Stars was built by a four-person team during the SFU Summer Summit Game Jam. We designed a 2D platformer about an astronaut escaping a wasted planet. Players draw temporary platforms that fade, so moving upward requires planning and urgency. I created art assets and designed a route that kept the mechanic readable without removing its difficulty.

We spent much of the first day and part of the second debating two directions: limited drawing ink or controllable falling pieces. Once the team committed to drawing, I established an orange industrial palette inspired by Blade Runner and mapped the climb in Aseprite. My sketches marked movement, ladders, fans, traps, moving platforms, decorations, and recovery points. Keeping the background visible beneath each pass helped us judge the full route before Unity.

The gameplay loop became read, draw, and climb. Dark, separated platform groups helped players scan for the next safe line. Jumping and creating ink both consumed energy, turning movement and drawing into limited resources. Green Nano Ink stayed distinct from the orange environment during testing, so we kept that contrast. As the programmer integrated mechanics, I adjusted spacing and hazards around what worked rather than protecting the original sketch.

Our final build was playable, but the schedule exposed problems. We lost time debating mechanics, left a large blank area near the top, and never implemented the fan activation button. Painting the entire map before checking the close camera also made pixels appear much larger than expected. I now prioritize a playable greybox, camera-scale tests, and an early scope decision before visual polish. The project demonstrates how I connect visual hierarchy, spatial rhythm, and iteration under pressure.
